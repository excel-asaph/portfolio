_______   PROJECT DETAILS

Understanding the project.

About

Road Crash Severity is an academic ML research project using FRSC quarterly road traffic data to predict whether a crash is fatal or non-fatal. The dataset covers 481 records spanning 37 Nigerian states and 13 quarters from Q3 2021 to Q3 2024. Five models were implemented and compared: three neural network configurations (unregularized, L1, and L2 with dropout), SVM, and XGBoost. SMOTE was applied to address a 477:4 class imbalance that would have made every model trivially predict "fatal" without it.

Problem Statement

Nigeria records 41,709 road fatalities annually, with speeding accounting for 56.3% of all recorded crashes. The FRSC national dataset provides state-level quarterly summaries of crash counts and causative factor breakdowns. The goal is to predict crash severity from these distributions. The catch is that the raw data is almost entirely labeled fatal: 477 records to 4, which makes model evaluation meaningless without addressing the imbalance first.

PROBLEM & CONTEXT

477 fatal crash records. 4 non-fatal ones. A model trained on that learns nothing except to always say fatal.

_______   THREE PROBLEMS INSIDE ONE

What made this harder than it looks.

The Imbalance Is a Data Collection Signal, Not Just a Technical Problem

A 477:4 split doesn't just mean the classes are unequal. It means the FRSC records fatal crashes more systematically than non-fatal ones. That's a reporting pattern, not purely a road safety reality. Before any model sees the data, you have to decide whether SMOTE is fixing a learning problem or papering over a collection problem. I chose SMOTE because the learning problem was real and addressable, but I kept the collection question in mind when interpreting results.

Tree Models Should Always Be Your Tabular Baseline

Neural networks get a lot of attention for their flexibility, but on tabular datasets with a few hundred rows, gradient boosting almost always wins. The inductive biases that make NNs powerful on images and sequences work against them when the dataset is small and structured. I ran three neural network configurations not because I expected them to beat XGBoost, but because the comparison itself was the research question: how do different regularization strategies respond when data is this limited?

97.2% Accuracy Means Less Than It Sounds Without Context

After SMOTE, 97.2% accuracy is a real result. Before SMOTE, predicting "fatal" for every single record gives 99.2%. The number only means something if the dataset it's measured on is honest. This is why SMOTE must be applied after the train-test split: if synthetic samples leak into the test set, the metrics describe how well the model learned from SMOTE's interpolation, not from real crash patterns.

_______   THE DATA

481 records, 37 states, 13 quarters.

The FRSC Road Transport Data covers quarterly crash summaries across all 37 Nigerian states from Q3 2021 to Q3 2024. The raw Excel file stores crash statistics and causative factor data as two separate blocks at different row positions, which had to be extracted independently and joined on STATE and QUARTER before any feature engineering. Missing values in the merged data were handled with linear interpolation within each state's time series, followed by quarter-level mean imputation for any remaining gaps. I converted raw counts to percentage-based features to make them comparable across states and time periods with different total crash volumes. The target is a binary severity label: fatal if any fatalities were recorded in that state-quarter combination, non-fatal otherwise.

_______   PIPELINE ARCHITECTURE

From raw FRSC data to trained model.

The pipeline starts with raw FRSC quarterly data, applies feature engineering to normalize crash counts into percentage distributions, then splits into train and test sets before SMOTE is applied. The order matters: SMOTE runs on the training set only, after the split, so synthetic samples never appear in the test data. After SMOTE the dataset grew from 481 to 954 samples, split 70/15/15 into 667 training, 143 validation, and 144 test records. All five models train on the same balanced set and are evaluated on the same 143-sample validation split.

RESULTS & EXECUTION

_______   MODEL COMPARISON

XGBoost 97.2%, perfect precision.

All five models were evaluated on the same unbalanced validation split after training on SMOTE-balanced data. XGBoost delivered the strongest performance across every metric: 97.2% accuracy, 0.9733 F1, and 100% precision. The L2-regularized neural network with dropout was the best-performing NN at 91.6% accuracy. The most instructive failure was NN2 with L1 regularization: a learning rate of 0.5 combined with aggressive L1 at 0.05 destabilized training so thoroughly that the model predicted FATAL for every single record. It achieved 100% recall for the fatal class and precision=0.00 for non-fatal — not because it learned anything, but because it found the trivially safe prediction and never escaped it.

_______   WHY XGBOOST WON

Small data, tabular structure, SMOTE: a gradient boosting problem.

Tree-based models outperform neural networks on small tabular datasets because their inductive biases fit the problem structure more naturally. XGBoost's gradient boosting iteratively corrects prediction errors from previous trees, which makes it robust to the kinds of feature distributions you get from percentage-normalized FRSC records. Its hyperparameters were tuned conservatively: max_depth=3 prevents overfitting on synthetic SMOTE samples, subsample=0.5 introduces randomness that reduces variance, and the low learning rate of 0.02 with 100 estimators builds the ensemble carefully. The SVM's poor result with the sigmoid kernel is also informative: sigmoid kernels behave similarly to neural networks on SMOTE data, which is why they struggled alongside the neural networks while XGBoost did not.

LEARNINGS

_______   KEY FINDINGS

What I took away from this research.

"477:4 wasn't a data quality problem. It was a data collection signal: FRSC documents fatal crashes more consistently than non-fatal ones. That tells you something about the reporting system before it tells you anything about road safety. I'd want to understand why the imbalance is that extreme before relying on any model's predictions to inform policy decisions."

What worked well

SMOTE resolved the class imbalance cleanly enough that a real five-model comparison became possible. Without it, every model would converge to predicting "fatal" for everything and the study would produce no insight. With it, the regularization differences between the three neural network configurations became visible and interpretable: you can actually see the effect of L1 collapsing to pure recall prediction, while L2 with dropout holds stable at 91.6%.

The XGBoost result at 97.2% with 100% precision is the strongest number in the project, and the most meaningful part of it is the gap between XGBoost and the best neural network. That 6-percentage-point difference, on a dataset with 954 records after SMOTE, clarified something about how gradient boosting and deep learning relate to tabular data at small scale that I hadn't fully internalized before running these experiments.

What I would do differently

The most significant limitation is the data itself. 481 records of aggregated state-level quarterly data is a thin basis for a binary classifier, especially when 4 of those records represent the entire non-fatal class before SMOTE. Individual crash-level data, if it were accessible through FRSC, would change both the feature set and what you can actually conclude from the results. With crash-level records you could include road type, time of day, vehicle type, and driver demographics rather than percentage distributions of causative factors across an entire state for an entire quarter.

I would also add SHAP values to the XGBoost output. The model currently produces a severity prediction but no explanation for why that prediction was made. SHAP values would show which causative factors most influenced each prediction and would let you trace whether speeding or brake failure is the dominant predictor per state. That kind of interpretability is what makes a research model actually useful for road safety policy rather than just academically interesting.
