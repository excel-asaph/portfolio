_______   PROJECT DETAILS

Understanding the project.

About

Sparr AI is a voice-first AI mock interview simulator. Candidates upload their resume, select a target company and role, choose from 5 AI interview personas (each with a distinct voice and personality), then complete a live voice interview. The system generates a detailed report with scored performance, timestamped audio feedback, and specific improvement recommendations. Interviews can be linked into Session Spaces that escalate difficulty based on prior weaknesses.

Problem Statement

Traditional interview prep tools are polite, scripted, and predictable. They give the same generic feedback regardless of the candidate's background, tell you "good job" when your answer is mediocre, and never simulate what a real FAANG technical round or a startup culture-fit conversation actually feels like under pressure. The result: candidates walk into real interviews having practiced only for the ideal version of a conversation that rarely exists.

_______   KEY OBSERVATIONS

The gap between practice and reality.

Research context
The motivation for Sparr came from real conversations, not data. Before a single line of code was written, I spent time talking to peers going through job applications: final-year students at ALU, bootcamp graduates, people making career transitions. The same pattern kept coming up. Technically capable people were failing not because they lacked knowledge, but because they had never practiced under real pressure. I watched friends do mock interviews with each other. The feedback was always gentle, always polite. Nobody pushed back on an answer. Nobody challenged a technical claim on their resume. And then they'd walk into a James-type interview and fall apart. Most prep tools optimize for comfort. Real interviewers optimize for signal. Sparr was built specifically for that gap.
The Candidate's Trap

In almost every conversation I had, candidates described some version of the same story: they'd practiced every common question, felt prepared, walked in, and completely unraveled the moment an interviewer pushed back. They'd rehearsed answers to "Tell me about yourself" but had never dealt with a skeptical senior engineer who immediately challenges the technical claims in their resume, or an HR interviewer who subtly probes for inconsistencies in their behavioral stories. The safe, affirming environment of most tools trains the wrong muscle: comfort, not composure under pressure.

The Interviewer's Diversity

Real interview panels are not homogeneous. In a single day a candidate might face a casual peer culture-fit conversation, a deeply technical algorithm discussion in a language they barely know, an HR behavioral screen, and a pragmatic startup founder who just wants to know if you can ship. Each requires a completely different communication style, level of formality, and type of evidence. No single AI persona can simulate all of these. So Sparr ships five, each with a distinct ElevenLabs voice, personality, and evaluation focus.

The Engineering Insight

The most valuable thing an interview simulator can do is not ask better questions. It's to remember what the candidate got wrong last time and push harder there. Static practice is additive: every session is a fresh start. Real interviewers (especially at large companies with looped hiring) do look at past performance signals. Sparr's Session Spaces architecture uses Firestore linked-list chains to create interview continuity, where each new session inherits the context of previous weaknesses, making the AI progressively harder to satisfy on the areas that matter most.

Questions that shaped the design
Every feature in Sparr answers a specific question that generic prep tools leave unanswered.
''
How does a prep tool know which specific parts of YOUR resume a real interviewer would probe?
''
How do you practice for a FAANG technical screen AND a startup culture-fit in the same tool without them feeling identical?
''
What does "getting better at interviews" actually look like over time, and how do you measure it?
''
Can an AI interviewer sound genuinely different from a chatbot, natural enough that the pressure of the conversation feels real?
''
How do you give feedback that's specific to what the candidate said, not generic performance advice they could find on any blog?
''
How do you make an interview simulator work for candidates across different languages without losing the personality of each persona?

_______   COMPETITIVE ANALYSIS

What candidates use today.

Three tools come up most often when candidates talk about interview prep. Each solves part of the problem. None of them solve the part that matters most.
Peer-to-Peer Mock Interviews
Free mock interview platform where candidates are matched with each other. Popular for technical coding preparation.
Pros
  • Real human interviewers, live conversation
  • Free to use, includes a coding environment
  • Covers DSA, system design, and behavioral rounds
Cons
  • Quality depends entirely on the stranger you're matched with
  • No resume-awareness; questions are not tied to your background
  • No session continuity across practice rounds
The gap "Practice quality depends entirely on who shows up."
AI-Powered Interview Training
Structured video interview platform with a large question library, AI feedback on communication, and performance scoring.
Pros
  • Large question bank organized by industry and role
  • Structured scoring across multiple dimensions
  • Good for behavioral and communication prep
Cons
  • Questions are not based on your resume or target company
  • No real voice pressure; text or video prompts only
  • No session memory or progressive difficulty
The gap "You rehearse answers to common questions, not the specific probes a real interviewer would use on you."
AI Speech Coach
An AI coach that joins your practice sessions and gives real-time feedback on pacing, filler words, and communication clarity.
Pros
  • Strong real-time delivery coaching
  • Tracks communication patterns over time
  • Easy to layer on top of existing practice
Cons
  • Focuses on how you speak, not what you say
  • No technical depth, no persona-based pressure
  • No resume intelligence or session memory
The gap "Teaches you to communicate more clearly. Doesn't teach you to think clearly under pressure."

_______   SESSION SPACES

The AI that remembers what you got wrong.

Session Spaces was the feature I was most excited to build. Interviews can be linked into chains using Firestore's parentId/childId document references, creating a persistent session memory where each follow-up interview inherits the context of previous performance and deliberately revisits areas where the candidate underperformed.
1
First Interview (Root Session)
Candidate completes interview with James (Skeptic Senior). Report identifies weakness: System Design explanations too high-level.
2
Follow-Up Session (childId linked)
New session created with parentId pointing to session 1. Gemini reads the prior report, and James probes system design harder, earlier, with less patience.
3
Progression Tracked in Firestore
Each session in a Space is visualized as a linked node in the ReactFlow workflow builder. Candidate sees their full interview history as a conversation flow.
∞
Escalating Difficulty
The chain doesn't reset. Every session builds on the last. The AI becomes harder to satisfy on weak areas. The candidate either improves, or stays exposed.

_______   RESUME-AWARE PROBING

The AI that reads your resume before judging it.

During onboarding, candidates upload their resume as a PDF. The backend extracts text via pdf-parse (2000 character window), then passes it to Gemini along with the target role and company context. Gemini returns 3–5 specific technical weaknesses it identifies in the resume relative to the job requirements. These weaknesses are then injected into the persona's dynamically-generated system prompt so the interviewer doesn't ask generic questions. It asks about the specific gaps between what you've claimed and what the role demands.
The intelligence pipeline
Step 1 — Resume parse

PDF uploaded to backend via Multer. pdf-parse extracts raw text. Gemini receives text, target role, company, and experience level.

Step 2 — Gap analysis

Gemini identifies 3–5 specific weaknesses: missing skills, shallow experience in key areas, or credentials that don't match the role requirements.

Step 3 — Prompt injection

Weaknesses are injected into the persona's system prompt alongside personality traits, questioning style, and language preference. ElevenLabs receives the final prompt and begins the interview.

_______   THE INTERVIEW REPORT

What Sparr delivers after every session.

The live interview interface
The interview window runs full-screen with a 3D animated orb (built in Three.js) serving as the visual focus while ElevenLabs streams audio in real time. The candidate's workbook is accessible as a slide-out panel for taking notes during the session. ElevenLabs is configured with an eager turn-taking mode: 7 seconds of silence triggers a response prompt, 20 seconds auto-ends the candidate's turn. Maximum interview duration is 5 minutes per session, keeping each session focused and preventing the sprawling conversations that make transcripts hard to analyse.
Sparr — Live interview session
Report generation
After each interview, the full conversation transcript is sent to Gemini for analysis. The generated report includes timestamped feedback linked to specific moments in the audio, a radar chart scoring the candidate across 6–8 dimensions (communication, technical depth, problem-solving, composure, etc.), a waveform timeline with highlighted feedback moments, and actionable improvement recommendations. I also wired up PDF export so candidates can save and share their reports.
Sparr — Interview Report
Performance dashboard
The progress dashboard aggregates scores across all sessions, tracking improvement over time across performance dimensions. Candidates can see not just how they did in a single interview, but how their composure, technical depth, and communication scores trend across a series of sessions within a Space.

LEARNINGS

_______   KEY FINDINGS

Measuring success.

"The most effective thing Sparr does is not ask harder questions. It asks the right questions to the right person. When James reads your resume and immediately challenges the one Django project where your role was ambiguous, that's not a generic interview. That's a conversation you've never had the chance to practice for."

What worked well

The ElevenLabs integration performed better than expected. Sub-second voice latency made conversations feel genuinely natural, unlike the stilted turn-taking you get from most voice AI tools. The candidates and engineers who used Sparr during and after the hackathon kept coming back to one thing: Ellen, specifically, felt like a real technical interview, not a chatbot. That was exactly the validation the persona-specific system prompt approach needed. The dynamic prompt approach, where Gemini crafts a fresh persona instruction from scratch for each session by pulling from resume analysis, company context, and prior session data, consistently outperformed the static prompts we tried early on. Every interview felt different.

The Firestore linked-list approach for Session Spaces was clean to implement and scale. I chose parentId/childId document references specifically because it meant no complex graph database was needed. Firestore's document model handled the chain structure naturally. The ReactFlow visualization of the interview chain was one of the features I was most proud of, and it added genuine value beyond aesthetics: candidates could see exactly how their sessions were connected and where they'd been challenged before.

What I would do differently

A lot of Sparr's architectural decisions were made fast. We were building under hackathon constraints, and speed forced clarity but also created technical debt. The most obvious place it shows is the resume parsing pipeline. I chose pdf-parse because it was quick to integrate, and it handles most PDF formats well enough. But heavily formatted resumes (tables, columns, custom fonts) produce messy extracted text that confuses Gemini's gap analysis. With more time, I'd swap it for a dedicated document intelligence service like Google Document AI. I'd also parse by section (Experience, Skills, Projects) rather than by raw character count, which would give Gemini a much more structured context to work from.

The report generation could be significantly richer. Right now it produces a single-pass analysis of the full transcript after the interview ends. A better approach would process the transcript progressively during the interview itself, tagging specific moments in real time and building the report as the conversation unfolds. That would also open the door to live coaching, nudging the candidate mid-interview when an answer is going off track, which is something none of the current tools do.
